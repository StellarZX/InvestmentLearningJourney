# Lesson 05 — What Is an ETF?

> *"You cannot buy an index, but you can buy an ETF that follows it."*

---

# Introduction

In the previous chapter, we learned that market indices are one of the best ways to understand the overall performance of a stock market.

For example,

you can easily follow:

- the CSI 300,
- the S&P 500,
- the NASDAQ-100,
- or the Hang Seng Index.

However, a new question immediately appears.

If an index is only a number,

how can we invest in it?

Can we buy the CSI 300?

Can we own the S&P 500?

The answer is no.

A market index is only a mathematical calculation.

It is not a financial product.

This is exactly why ETFs were created.

---

# What Is an ETF?

ETF stands for **Exchange-Traded Fund**.

It is an investment fund that is traded on a stock exchange, just like a stock.

The main goal of an ETF is to follow the performance of a particular index or group of assets.

Instead of buying every company yourself,

you buy one ETF.

The ETF does the work for you.

For example,

suppose an ETF tracks the S&P 500.

When you buy one share of this ETF,

you indirectly own small portions of hundreds of American companies.

You do not need to purchase Apple,

Microsoft,

Amazon,

or NVIDIA separately.

The ETF combines them into a single investment product.

---

# Why Were ETFs Created?

Imagine that you want to invest in the S&P 500 by yourself.

The index contains about 500 companies.

To copy the index perfectly,

you would need to:

- buy shares of every company,
- keep the correct proportions,
- rebalance your portfolio regularly,
- pay hundreds of trading commissions.

For an ordinary investor,

this would be almost impossible.

ETFs solve this problem.

Professional fund managers purchase and maintain all of the underlying assets.

Individual investors only need to buy one ETF.

As a result,

investing becomes much simpler,

less expensive,

and more efficient.

---

# How Does an ETF Track an Index?

The purpose of an ETF is not to outperform an index.

Its purpose is to follow the index as closely as possible.

Suppose the CSI 300 rises by 3%.

A CSI 300 ETF should also rise by approximately 3%.

Likewise,

if the S&P 500 falls by 2%,

an ETF tracking the S&P 500 should also fall by roughly 2%.

The ETF manager regularly adjusts the fund's holdings to ensure that the ETF continues to match the index.

This process is called **tracking**.

Although the ETF and the index are not exactly identical,

their long-term performance should be very similar.

---

# One Index, Many ETFs

Many beginners are surprised to discover that there are several ETFs tracking exactly the same index.

For example,

there are multiple ETFs tracking the CSI 300.

Why?

Because different fund companies can create products that follow the same index.

Although these ETFs have different names,

their investment objective is the same.

Their long-term performance should therefore be very similar.

The main differences usually include:

- management fees,
- fund size,
- liquidity,
- and tracking accuracy.

The underlying index remains exactly the same.

This is why experienced investors usually choose the index first,

and the ETF second.

---

# Why Are ETFs So Popular?

Over the past two decades,

ETFs have become one of the most popular investment products in the world.

There are several reasons.

First,

they provide instant diversification.

Buying one ETF often gives you exposure to hundreds of companies.

Second,

their management fees are usually much lower than actively managed mutual funds.

Third,

they are easy to trade because they are listed on stock exchanges.

Finally,

they allow ordinary investors to participate in long-term market growth without selecting individual companies.

For many beginners,

ETFs provide a simple and practical starting point for investing.

---

# ETF Is a Tool, Not a Strategy

This is one of the most important ideas in this book.

Many people ask,

> "Which ETF should I buy?"

A better question is,

> "Which market do I want to invest in?"

An ETF is only a vehicle.

The investment decision is determined by the index it follows.

For example,

buying an S&P 500 ETF means that you believe in the long-term growth of large American companies.

Buying a CSI 300 ETF means that you believe in the long-term development of China's largest listed companies.

Therefore,

when evaluating an ETF,

always ask yourself:

> **What does this ETF actually own?**

rather than

> **Which company issued this ETF?**

---

# Summary

An ETF is an investment fund that trades on a stock exchange.

Unlike a market index,

an ETF can be bought and sold by investors.

Most ETFs are designed to track the performance of an index.

Instead of purchasing hundreds of individual stocks,

investors only need to buy a single ETF.

This makes investing simpler,

more diversified,

and more accessible.

Remember one important principle:

**Choose the index first, then choose the ETF.**

The ETF is only the tool.

The index is the real investment.
