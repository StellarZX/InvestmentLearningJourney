# Lesson 24 — Tracking Error

> *"An ETF follows an index, but following does not always mean matching perfectly."*

---

# Introduction

In the previous lesson, we learned that fees reduce fund returns.

Fees are one reason an ETF may perform slightly differently from its index.

This difference leads us to tracking error.

Tracking error describes how closely a fund follows its benchmark index.

For an index ETF, the goal is usually to keep tracking error low.

The investor expects the ETF to behave like the index it tracks.

---

## Why ETFs Do Not Match Perfectly

An index is a calculation.

An ETF is a real fund that must buy, hold, and trade securities.

Because of this, perfect matching is difficult.

The fund has operating expenses.

It may receive dividends at different times.

It may need to trade when the index changes.

It may hold a sample instead of every security in the index.

It may also face taxes, trading costs, and market liquidity limits.

All these factors can create small differences between the ETF and the index.

---

## Sources of Tracking Error

The first source is fees.

Because the fund pays costs, its return is usually slightly lower than the index before considering other effects.

The second source is trading cost.

When an index changes, the ETF may need to buy and sell securities.

Those trades have costs.

The third source is sampling.

If an ETF does not hold every security in the index, its performance may differ from the full index.

The fourth source is timing.

Dividends, rebalancing, and market holidays can create small timing differences.

---

## Tracking Difference and Tracking Error

Beginners may see two related ideas.

Tracking difference is the return difference between the ETF and the index over a period.

Tracking error measures how much that difference moves over time.

For most beginners, the main idea is simple:

a good index ETF should follow its index closely and consistently.

The investor buys the ETF to get index exposure, not an unexpected result.

---

## Why Tracking Error Matters

If you buy an ETF to track the S&P 500, you expect S&P 500-like results.

If the ETF performs very differently, your plan may not work as expected.

Tracking error matters more when the ETF invests in difficult markets, narrow sectors, or complex strategies.

Broad, large, liquid ETFs often track more closely.

Before choosing an ETF, investors can compare its past performance with its index.

Past tracking does not guarantee future tracking, but it gives useful information.

---

## Summary

Tracking error shows how closely an ETF follows its benchmark index.

Differences can come from fees, trading costs, sampling, timing, taxes, and market conditions.

Low tracking error is usually desirable for a passive index ETF.

Investors buy index ETFs because they want the index exposure, not an unexpected result.

In the next lesson, we will study liquidity.

Even a good ETF can be inconvenient if it is hard to buy or sell at a fair price.
