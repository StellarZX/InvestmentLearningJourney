# Lesson 23 — Expense Ratio

> *"A small fee can become a large cost when time is long."*

---

# Introduction

ETFs are popular partly because many of them are low-cost.

But low-cost does not mean free.

Every fund needs people, systems, custody, trading, reporting, and management.

The expense ratio is the annual fee charged by a fund to operate.

For long-term investors, fees matter because they reduce returns every year.

A difference that looks tiny in one year can become meaningful over decades.

---

## What Is an Expense Ratio?

The expense ratio is shown as a percentage of the money invested in the fund.

If an ETF has an expense ratio of 0.20%, it means the annual fund cost is about 0.20% of assets.

Investors usually do not pay this fee as a separate bill.

It is taken from the fund's assets over time.

This makes the fee easy to ignore.

But it still affects the investor's return.

If two ETFs track the same index and one has a much higher expense ratio, the higher-cost ETF has a disadvantage.

---

## Why Fees Matter Over Time

Fees reduce the amount of money that remains invested.

Because compounding works on the money that stays invested, fees also reduce future compounding.

Imagine two similar funds.

One charges 0.10% per year.

The other charges 1.00% per year.

In one year, the difference may not feel huge.

Over twenty or thirty years, the difference can become large.

The investor does not lose only the fee.

The investor also loses the future growth that the fee could have earned.

---

## Low Fees Are Not the Only Factor

Fees are important, but they are not the only factor.

An ETF should also track the right index.

It should have enough liquidity.

It should be issued by a reliable fund provider.

It should fit the investor's portfolio.

A low fee does not make a bad investment good.

Cost matters most when comparing similar funds.

If two ETFs track the same index and have similar liquidity, the lower expense ratio is usually better.

---

## Active Funds and Passive ETFs

Many active funds charge higher fees because they pay managers to select securities and try to beat the market.

Some active managers may succeed for a period.

But it is difficult to outperform consistently after fees.

Passive ETFs often charge lower fees because they follow an index rather than making many active decisions.

This is one reason passive investing has become popular.

Investors can own the market at a low cost.

---

## Summary

The expense ratio is the annual cost of owning a fund.

It is usually taken from the fund's assets, so investors may not notice it directly.

Fees reduce returns and reduce future compounding.

Low fees are especially important for long-term investors.

However, cost should be considered together with index choice, liquidity, provider quality, and portfolio fit.

In the next lesson, we will study tracking error.

Even when an ETF is designed to follow an index, it may not match the index perfectly.
