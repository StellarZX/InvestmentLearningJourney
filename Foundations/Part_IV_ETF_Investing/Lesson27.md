# Lesson 27 — Portfolio Construction

> *"A portfolio is not a pile of products. It is a plan expressed through assets."*

---

# Introduction

By now, we understand ETFs, fees, tracking, liquidity, and regular investing.

The next question is practical:

how can a beginner build a portfolio?

Portfolio construction means choosing investments and deciding how they work together.

It begins with goals, not products.

Before asking which ETF to buy, an investor should ask what the money is for, when it may be needed, and how much risk is acceptable.

---

## Start With the Goal

Different goals need different portfolios.

Money for an emergency fund should be safe and liquid.

Money for a house down payment in two years should not depend heavily on stocks.

Money for retirement in thirty years can usually accept more volatility.

The time horizon matters because stocks can fall sharply in the short term but have stronger long-term growth potential.

A good portfolio fits the purpose of the money.

If the purpose is unclear, the portfolio may become confused.

---

## Choosing ETFs

For beginners, broad index ETFs are often easier than narrow thematic ETFs.

A broad stock ETF may hold hundreds or thousands of companies.

A bond ETF may provide exposure to government or corporate bonds.

An international ETF may add global diversification.

When comparing ETFs, investors can look at the index, expense ratio, tracking history, liquidity, fund size, and provider.

The goal is not to own many ETFs.

The goal is to own the right exposure in a simple way.

---

## Asset Allocation Examples

A young long-term investor may choose a high stock allocation.

This type of portfolio has higher growth potential but also larger declines.

A more conservative investor may hold a larger bond allocation.

This may reduce volatility but also reduce expected return.

An investor with near-term goals may hold more cash or short-term bond funds.

These examples are not recommendations.

They show how allocation changes with goals and risk tolerance.

The correct mix is personal.

---

## Portfolio Rebalancing

Over time, a portfolio changes because assets grow at different speeds.

Suppose an investor chooses 70% stocks and 30% bonds.

If stocks rise strongly, the portfolio may become 80% stocks and 20% bonds.

This means the portfolio is now riskier than planned.

Rebalancing means bringing the portfolio back to its target allocation.

This can be done by selling some assets that grew too much, buying assets that became smaller, or directing new contributions to the underweight asset.

---

## Summary

Portfolio construction begins with goals and time horizon.

ETFs should be chosen for their role in the portfolio, not because they are popular.

Asset allocation determines the main risk and return pattern.

Rebalancing keeps the portfolio close to the plan.
