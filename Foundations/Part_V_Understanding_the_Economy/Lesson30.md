# Lesson 30 — Valuation

> *"A good company can still be a poor investment if the price is too high."*

---

# Introduction

Financial statements help us understand a business.

Valuation helps us understand the price investors are paying for that business.

This matters because investment return depends not only on quality, but also on price.

A strong company can be overvalued.

A weak company can look cheap for a reason.

Valuation is not an exact science.

It is a way to think about expectations, risk, and future returns.

---

## Price-to-Earnings Ratio

The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E ratio, compares a company's stock price with its earnings per share.

In simple terms, it asks how much investors are paying for one unit of profit.

A high P/E ratio often means investors expect strong future growth.

A low P/E ratio may mean the market has low expectations or sees higher risk.

P/E is useful, but it has limits.

Some companies have temporarily low earnings.

Some fast-growing companies may have high P/E ratios for many years.

The ratio should be compared with business quality, growth, industry, and interest rates.

---

## Price-to-Book Ratio

The price-to-book ratio, or P/B ratio, compares market value with book value.

Book value is based on the company's assets minus liabilities on the balance sheet.

P/B is often used for banks, insurance companies, and asset-heavy businesses.

It is less useful for companies whose value comes mainly from software, brands, or networks.

For example, a technology company may have valuable intellectual property that does not fully appear on the balance sheet.

This is why one ratio cannot explain every business.

---

## Price-to-Sales Ratio

The price-to-sales ratio, or P/S ratio, compares market value with revenue.

It can be useful when a company has little or no profit yet.

Some young growth companies may spend heavily today to grow faster in the future.

In that case, earnings may be low or negative, so P/E is not useful.

However, revenue alone is not enough.

A company must eventually turn sales into profit and cash flow.

A high P/S ratio can be risky if future profits do not arrive.

---

## Enterprise Value

Enterprise value, or EV, measures the value of a company including debt and cash.

It is often used when comparing companies with different capital structures.

Two companies may have similar market values, but one may have much more debt.

EV helps investors see the total value of the business more clearly.

It is often compared with sales, earnings before interest and taxes, or cash flow.

Beginners do not need to use EV deeply at first.

But they should know that debt matters when judging valuation.

---

## Why Valuation Matters

Valuation matters because price affects future return.

If investors pay a very high price, the future must be very good to justify it.

If expectations are too optimistic, returns may disappoint even if the company grows.

For broad ETFs, valuation can also matter.

An index with very high valuation may have lower future expected returns than an index with reasonable valuation.

This does not mean investors can predict short-term market movements.

It means valuation is one part of long-term thinking.

---

## Summary

Valuation helps investors understand the price paid for a business or market.

P/E compares price with earnings.

P/B compares price with book value.

P/S compares price with revenue.

Enterprise value includes debt and cash.

No ratio is perfect.

Valuation should be used with business quality, growth, risk, and time horizon.

In the next lesson, we will study economic cycles.

Valuation helps us understand price, while cycles help us understand the environment around companies.
