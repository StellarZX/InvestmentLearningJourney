# Lesson 29 — Financial Statements

> *"A stock price is only the surface. A business has numbers underneath."*

---

# Introduction

In the ETF part, we focused on investment products and portfolios.

Now we move closer to the real economy.

Even if a beginner mainly invests through ETFs, it is still useful to understand how companies report their business.

Financial statements help investors see what is happening inside a company.

They do not tell the whole story, but they provide a starting point.

The three main statements are the balance sheet, the income statement, and the cash flow statement.

---

## Balance Sheet

The balance sheet shows what a company owns and what it owes at a specific point in time.

What a company owns is called assets.

Examples include cash, inventory, factories, equipment, and investments.

What a company owes is called liabilities.

Examples include bank loans, bonds, unpaid bills, and other obligations.

The difference between assets and liabilities is shareholders' equity.

In simple terms, the balance sheet answers:

what does the company have, and how much does it owe?

---

## Income Statement

The income statement shows a company's revenue, expenses, and profit over a period of time.

Revenue is the money earned from selling products or services.

Expenses are the costs needed to run the business.

Profit is what remains after expenses.

For example, Apple sells iPhones, Macs, and services.

Its income statement helps investors see how much revenue it earns and how much profit remains after costs.

A company can have high revenue but low profit if expenses are also high.

This is why investors look beyond sales alone.

---

## Cash Flow Statement

Profit and cash are not always the same.

A company may report profit but still have weak cash flow.

The cash flow statement shows how cash enters and leaves the business.

Operating cash flow comes from the main business.

Investing cash flow relates to buying or selling long-term assets.

Financing cash flow relates to borrowing money, repaying debt, issuing shares, or paying dividends.

For long-term investors, cash flow is important because companies need real cash to survive, invest, and reward shareholders.

---

## Why Beginners Should Care

Beginners do not need to become accounting experts.

But they should understand that a stock represents a real business.

Financial statements help investors connect stock prices with business reality.

They also help investors understand why indices and ETFs change over time.

If many companies in an index grow revenue and profits, the index may become more valuable over the long term.

If companies face falling profits and weak cash flow, the market may become more cautious.

---

## Summary

Financial statements help investors understand companies.

The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity.

The income statement shows revenue, expenses, and profit.

The cash flow statement shows how cash moves through the business.

Even ETF investors benefit from understanding these basics because ETFs ultimately own real companies.

Financial statements show the business; valuation asks how much investors are paying for it.
