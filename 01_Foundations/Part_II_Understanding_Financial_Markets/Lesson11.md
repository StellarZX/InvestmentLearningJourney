# Lesson 11 — Bull Markets & Bear Markets

> *"Markets move in cycles, but investor emotions often move even more dramatically."*

---

# Introduction

If you invest in stock markets for a long time,

you will often hear two expressions:

* "We are in a bull market."
* "The market has entered a bear market."

These two terms are very common in financial news.

When prices keep rising,

people become excited.

When prices keep falling,

people become afraid.

But bull markets and bear markets are not just about price movements.

They are also about investor confidence,

economic expectations,

and human psychology.

Understanding bull and bear markets is important for every investor.

It helps us avoid emotional decisions,

especially during periods of market excitement or panic.

---

# What Is a Bull Market?

A **bull market** refers to a period when stock prices are generally rising.

In a bull market,

investors are optimistic.

They believe that the economy will grow,

companies will make more profits,

and stock prices will continue to increase.

A common rule of thumb is that a bull market begins when a market rises by **20% or more** from a recent low.

For example,

if a stock index rises from 3,000 points to 3,600 points,

that is a 20% increase.

Bull markets are often supported by:

* Economic growth
* Rising company earnings
* Low interest rates
* Strong investor confidence
* Good market liquidity

During a bull market,

many investors feel comfortable investing.

Their portfolios rise,

financial news becomes positive,

and more people begin to enter the market.

However,

bull markets can also be dangerous.

When prices rise too quickly,

investors may become overconfident.

They may believe that the market will continue rising forever.

This often leads people to buy at expensive prices.

---

# What Is a Bear Market?

A **bear market** refers to a period when stock prices are generally falling.

In a bear market,

investors are pessimistic.

They worry about recession,

falling company profits,

high inflation,

high interest rates,

or financial crises.

A common rule of thumb is that a bear market begins when a market falls by **20% or more** from a recent high.

For example,

if a stock index falls from 4,000 points to 3,200 points,

that is a 20% decline.

Bear markets are often caused by:

* Economic slowdown
* Financial crisis
* High inflation
* Rising interest rates
* Falling corporate profits
* Loss of investor confidence

During a bear market,

many investors feel anxious.

Their portfolios decline,

financial news becomes negative,

and some people sell their investments because they are afraid of further losses.

However,

bear markets are a normal part of investing.

For long-term investors,

a bear market can also create opportunities to buy good assets at lower prices.

---

# Historical Examples

Bull and bear markets have appeared many times in history.

They are not unusual events.

They are part of the normal cycle of financial markets.

One famous example is the **dot-com bubble** in the late 1990s.

At that time,

many technology and internet companies became extremely popular.

Investors believed that the internet would change the world.

This belief was correct in the long run.

However,

many companies were priced too high and did not have strong profits.

When expectations became unrealistic,

the market eventually crashed.

This shows an important lesson:

> A good industry does not always mean a good investment price.

Another famous example is the **global financial crisis** of 2008.

During this period,

banks,

real estate markets,

and credit systems faced serious problems.

Stock markets around the world fell sharply.

Many investors were afraid and sold their investments.

However,

after the crisis,

markets gradually recovered.

Investors who stayed disciplined and continued investing were eventually rewarded.

A more recent example is the **COVID-19 market crash** in 2020.

At the beginning of the pandemic,

global markets fell very quickly.

Many investors feared that the world economy would stop for a long time.

But later,

with government support,

central bank policies,

and economic recovery expectations,

many markets recovered faster than expected.

These examples show that markets can be very emotional in the short term.

But over the long term,

markets are mainly driven by economic growth,

company earnings,

and investor expectations.

---

# Investor Psychology

Bull and bear markets are closely connected to human emotions.

The two most important emotions in investing are:

* Greed
* Fear

During a bull market,

greed becomes stronger.

Investors see prices rising,

and they do not want to miss the opportunity.

They may think:

> "Everyone is making money. I should buy more."

This is called **fear of missing out**, or FOMO.

When many people feel this way,

prices may rise even faster.

But the higher prices become,

the greater the risk may be.

During a bear market,

fear becomes stronger.

Investors see prices falling,

and they worry that losses will continue.

They may think:

> "The market will never recover. I should sell now."

This is called panic selling.

Many investors make their biggest mistakes during these emotional moments.

They buy aggressively when prices are high,

and sell in panic when prices are low.

In other words,

they do the opposite of what successful long-term investing requires.

A simple market psychology cycle looks like this:

```text
Optimism → Excitement → Euphoria → Anxiety → Fear → Panic → Depression → Hope → Optimism
```

This cycle repeats again and again in financial markets.

The names of companies change.

The news headlines change.

But human emotions remain very similar.

---

# How Should Index Fund Investors Respond?

For index fund investors,

the most important thing is not to predict every bull market or bear market.

It is almost impossible to know exactly when a market will reach the top or the bottom.

Instead,

index fund investors should focus on discipline.

In a bull market,

investors should avoid becoming too excited.

A rising market does not mean risk has disappeared.

If prices become too expensive,

future returns may become lower.

In a bear market,

investors should avoid panic selling.

A falling market does not mean investing has failed.

If the long-term fundamentals are still strong,

lower prices may provide better future opportunities.

Many long-term investors use simple strategies to reduce emotional decisions.

For example:

* Regular investing
* Diversification
* Asset allocation
* Rebalancing
* Valuation-based investing

These strategies do not remove risk completely.

But they help investors avoid making decisions based only on fear or greed.

For beginners,

this is especially important.

The goal is not to be the smartest person in the market.

The goal is to survive market cycles,

control emotions,

and continue investing with a clear plan.

---

# Summary

A bull market is a period when stock prices are generally rising.

A bear market is a period when stock prices are generally falling.

A common rule of thumb is that a 20% rise from a recent low may indicate a bull market, while a 20% fall from a recent high may indicate a bear market.

Bull markets are usually full of optimism,

confidence,

and rising expectations.

Bear markets are usually full of fear,

uncertainty,

and falling expectations.

Historical examples such as the dot-com bubble,

the global financial crisis,

and the COVID-19 crash show that market cycles are normal.

For index fund investors,

the most important lesson is not to predict every market movement.

The most important lesson is to control emotions,

avoid buying only because of greed,

avoid selling only because of fear,

and follow a disciplined long-term strategy.
