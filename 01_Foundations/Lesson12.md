# Lesson 12 — Market Capitalization

> *"The size of a company changes how investors understand its risk and growth."*

---

# Introduction

In the previous lesson, we learned about bull markets and bear markets.

We saw that markets do not move in a straight line.

Sometimes prices rise for a long time.

Sometimes prices fall sharply.

But when we read financial news, we often see another kind of description:

* large-cap stocks
* mid-cap stocks
* small-cap stocks

These words are all related to one idea:

**market capitalization**.

Market capitalization helps investors understand the size of a company in the stock market.

It does not tell us whether a company is good or bad.

It does not tell us whether the stock is cheap or expensive.

But it gives us a basic starting point.

Before comparing companies,

we need to understand how big they are.

---

## What Is Market Capitalization?

**Market capitalization**, often called **market cap**, means the total market value of a company’s shares.

The formula is simple:

```text
Market Capitalization = Share Price × Number of Shares
```

For example,

if a company has 1 billion shares,

and each share is worth $50,

then its market capitalization is:

```text
1 billion × $50 = $50 billion
```

This means the stock market values the whole company at $50 billion.

Market cap is not the same as the company’s revenue.

It is not the same as profit.

It is also not the same as cash in the bank.

It is the value investors are currently willing to give to the company in the stock market.

Because share prices change every day,

market capitalization also changes every day.

---

## Why Market Cap Matters

Market cap matters because companies of different sizes often behave differently.

A very large company is usually more mature.

It may already have a strong business,

many customers,

and stable revenue.

Examples may include companies like Apple, Microsoft, or Tencent.

These companies are usually not small experiments anymore.

They are already important businesses.

Because of this,

large companies may be more stable than smaller companies.

But their fastest growth period may already be behind them.

A small company is different.

It may still be growing quickly.

It may have more room to expand.

But it may also face greater risk.

Its business model may not be fully proven.

Its profits may be unstable.

It may be more sensitive to changes in the economy.

So market cap helps investors ask a useful question:

**Am I investing in a mature company, a growing company, or a smaller company with higher uncertainty?**

---

## Large-Cap Stocks

**Large-cap stocks** are stocks of large companies.

There is no single global rule,

but large-cap companies are usually worth many billions of dollars.

They are often well-known companies with strong market positions.

Large-cap companies may have:

* stable business models;
* strong brands;
* large customer bases;
* easier access to financing;
* more experienced management.

For beginners,

large-cap stocks are often easier to understand because they are familiar.

Many market indices are also dominated by large-cap companies.

For example,

the S&P 500 includes many large American companies.

The Hang Seng Index includes many important Hong Kong-listed companies.

The CSI 300 includes large companies listed in mainland China.

Large-cap stocks are not risk-free.

Their prices can still fall during bear markets.

Even strong companies can make mistakes.

But compared with smaller companies,

large-cap companies are usually considered more stable.

---

## Mid-Cap Stocks

**Mid-cap stocks** are stocks of medium-sized companies.

They are usually smaller than large-cap companies,

but larger and more established than small-cap companies.

Mid-cap companies can be interesting because they may still have meaningful growth potential.

They may already have a proven business,

but they may not yet be global leaders.

In some cases,

a successful mid-cap company can grow into a large-cap company over time.

However,

mid-cap stocks may also be more volatile than large-cap stocks.

They may be more affected by competition,

economic changes,

or management decisions.

For long-term investors,

mid-cap stocks can provide a balance between growth and stability.

They are not as mature as large-cap stocks,

but they are usually less uncertain than small-cap stocks.

---

## Small-Cap Stocks

**Small-cap stocks** are stocks of smaller companies.

These companies may be young,

less well-known,

or focused on a narrow market.

Small-cap companies can sometimes grow very quickly.

If a small company becomes successful,

its stock may have strong long-term returns.

But the risks are also higher.

Small-cap companies may have:

* less stable revenue;
* weaker financial resources;
* smaller customer bases;
* higher competition risk;
* lower trading liquidity.

Liquidity means how easily a stock can be bought or sold without strongly affecting its price.

Small-cap stocks often have lower liquidity.

This can make their prices move more sharply.

For beginners,

small-cap stocks can be difficult to analyze.

They may require more research,

more patience,

and a stronger ability to accept volatility.

---

## Market Cap and Index Investing

Market capitalization is very important in index investing.

Many famous indices are **market-cap weighted**.

This means larger companies have a bigger weight in the index.

For example,

if a company is very large,

it may represent a larger part of an index.

If its stock price moves,

it can have a bigger effect on the index.

This is why large technology companies can strongly influence indices such as the S&P 500 or NASDAQ-100.

When you buy an ETF that tracks a market-cap weighted index,

you are usually investing more money in larger companies.

This is not necessarily good or bad.

It is simply how many major indices are designed.

For beginners,

this is important to understand.

Buying an index ETF does not mean every company receives the same weight.

In many cases,

the biggest companies receive the largest share of your investment.

---

## Market Cap Is Not the Same as Value

A common mistake is to think that a larger company is always a better investment.

This is not true.

Market cap tells us company size.

It does not tell us whether the stock is cheap.

It does not tell us whether the company will grow.

It does not tell us whether investors are paying too much.

A large company can be overvalued.

A small company can be undervalued.

A famous company can still be a poor investment if the price is too high.

A less famous company can be a good investment if its business is strong and the price is reasonable.

Market cap is only one piece of information.

It should not be used alone.

Later in this book,

we will learn about valuation,

such as price-to-earnings ratio and price-to-book ratio.

Those tools help investors think more carefully about price and value.

---

## Why Beginners Should Care

Beginners should care about market cap because it helps them understand portfolio risk.

A portfolio full of small-cap stocks may grow faster,

but it may also fall more sharply.

A portfolio focused on large-cap stocks may feel more stable,

but it may have less explosive growth.

An ETF tracking a broad market index usually includes many companies of different sizes,

but large companies often have the biggest weight.

Understanding market cap helps you know what you actually own.

This is one of the first steps toward becoming a rational investor.

You should not only ask:

"What ETF should I buy?"

You should also ask:

"What kinds of companies does this ETF hold?"

Are they mostly large companies?

Are there mid-cap companies?

Are there small-cap companies?

The answer affects both risk and return.

---

## Summary

Market capitalization means the total market value of a company’s shares.

It is calculated by multiplying share price by the number of shares.

Companies are often grouped into large-cap,

mid-cap,

and small-cap stocks.

Large-cap companies are usually more mature and stable.

Mid-cap companies may offer a balance between growth and stability.

Small-cap companies may have higher growth potential,

but also higher risk.

Market cap is especially important in index investing,

because many indices give larger companies a bigger weight.

However,

market cap does not tell us whether a stock is cheap or expensive.

It only tells us the size of the company in the stock market.

In the next lesson,

we will study **growth vs. value investing**.

This will help us understand another important question:

Should investors focus on companies with high growth,

or companies that may be undervalued?
